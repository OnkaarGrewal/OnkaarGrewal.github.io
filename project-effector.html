Overview

Our team (Fri-21) was tasked with designing a robotic end effector for a Q-Arm to automate the packaging of irregular warehouse items — sponges, bottles, anything without a consistent shape — to cut manual handling injury risk and speed up processing.

My primary role was Project Manager — running team discussions and resolving conflicts — but I also owned a chunk of the mechanical design and the software layer. My claw design leaned on surface area rather than a rigid pinch grip: a sponge and rubber-foam-lined claw that deforms around irregular shapes to hold them, angled to work within the Q-Arm's restricted approach kinematics.

On the software side, I wrote the lookup_products() function in Python — it takes a scanned barcode, cross-references it against a products.csv database, and returns the matched [name, price] pair the arm's sorting logic runs on.

Process & Challenges

The biggest issue wasn't the claw itself — it was that I'd approved the mechanical concept without validating it against the Q-Arm's actual kinematics. I assumed the arm could approach objects head-on; it can't. The Q-Arm's restricted joint range forces it to approach the table at a steep downward angle, and our first printed prototype couldn't sit flush against anything because of that. We had to re-engineer the whole claw assembly with a 50-degree offset to fix it.

On the planning side, my original Gantt chart assumed each milestone would complete without rework. It didn't account for the iteration time that unpredictable physical issues — grip failure, gear ratio, the angle problem above — actually needed, which pushed our timeline later than planned.

What I Learned

◆
Validate against the whole system, not just the conceptA mechanically sound claw design still fails if it doesn't account for the kinematic constraints of the machine holding it.
◆
Integration is continuous, not a final stepPhysical and software integration issues need testing throughout the build, not just at the end, or they surface too late to fix cheaply.
◆
Leading a team is a design constraint tooResolving scheduling and role conflicts as PM was as much a part of shipping the final device as any single mechanical decision.
